# Hygiene in the shared laundry room

For a healthy person, a normally maintained communal washing machine does not require a special barrier around clothing. Detergent, temperature, mechanical action, rinsing and complete drying together provide the normal hygienic result.

## Before loading

- Inspect the drum, door seal and detergent compartment. Choose another machine or report it if there is visible dirt, foreign material, standing foul-smelling water, mold or a strong unusual odor.
- Remove any visible residue with a disposable paper towel before touching laundry. Do not mix cleaning chemicals.
- Keep clean laundry off shared floors, machine tops and communal baskets. Use a personal washable hamper or bag.
- Wash or sanitize hands after touching shared handles and controls and before handling the finished laundry.

## Program and detergent

- Follow each textile's label. Ordinary outerwear is normally adequate at 30–40 °C with detergent.
- Wash robust underwear, towels, bedding and kitchen cloths at 60 °C when their labels permit it. A powdered heavy-duty detergent containing oxygen bleach gives extra hygienic action, but use it only for white or bleach-safe colorfast textiles.
- For dark and colored textiles, use a color detergent and the recorded safe temperature. Do not sacrifice the garment's care limit merely because the machine is shared.
- Do not overload the drum. Laundry needs room to move and rinse; a tightly packed drum works worse. Follow the program's lower load limit for synthetics and delicates.
- Remove laundry promptly and dry it completely. Damp textiles left together encourage odor and microbial growth.

## What does not solve communal-machine hygiene

- Mesh laundry bags protect delicate garments, straps and small items from mechanical damage. They are permeable and do not isolate clothing from wash water or microorganisms.
- Hygiene rinse or laundry disinfectant is unnecessary for routine washing by a healthy person. It is reserved for a specific infection-control need or professional medical advice and must be compatible with the textile and program.
- An empty hot cycle before every personal load is unnecessary and would add cost. If a machine is visibly contaminated or foul-smelling, do not use it; report it to the operator. If the machine has merely been unused for a long time and this can be established, a 60 °C cycle can reduce contamination, but the operator should maintain communal machines.

## Illness or unusual contamination

- Laundry contaminated with vomit, stool or other potentially infectious body fluids should be handled without shaking, kept separate until loading, washed at at least 60 °C with bleach-containing heavy-duty detergent when the label permits, and dried completely.
- If a textile cannot tolerate the required program, or if there is a highly contagious infection or significant immune suppression, obtain case-specific advice from a clinician, pharmacy or public-health service.

## Sources

- Germany's Federal Centre for Health Education infection-protection guidance: normal outerwear can use lower temperatures; towels, bedding, underwear and cleaning cloths should use at least 60 °C with bleach-containing heavy-duty detergent when compatible; routine hygiene rinse is unnecessary.
- German Environment Agency household-hygiene guidance: a 60 °C load with bleach-containing heavy-duty detergent after about every fifth low-temperature wash or every two weeks helps prevent machine biofilm; additional laundry disinfection is normally unnecessary in a healthy household.
- German Federal Institute for Risk Assessment: hygienic performance depends on temperature, detergent, pH, mechanics and soil; 60 °C heavy-duty washing is appropriate for strongly contaminated textiles, while routine 90 °C washing is generally unnecessary.
- US CDC community guidance: use the warmest suitable setting and dry laundry completely; ordinary machine washing with detergent is sufficient even when laundry from an ill person is washed with other items.
